\begin{abstract}
Causal representation learning aims to recover latent causal variables and
their graph from high-dimensional measurements collected across multiple
environments. Its identifiability theorems impose explicit preconditions on
the data: enough environments relative to the latent dimension, and enough
samples within each environment to resolve a mechanism shift. Reported
performance on public benchmarks has been persistently poor, and the field has
responded by developing better estimators. We ask a prior question: do the
benchmark datasets satisfy the preconditions at all? We audit eight datasets
widely used to evaluate causal representation learning and related
multi-environment methods, comprising six single-cell perturbation screens and
two functional neuroimaging corpora, using only counting and spectral
statistics computed before any model is fitted. Three findings emerge. First,
the observed dimension of the unperturbed control covariance is 234 to 456 by
participation ratio and 496 to 1725 by 95 per cent variance at a matched
sample size of 2000 cells; a feature-count control rules out the obvious
artefactual explanation, since one screen has four times as many measured
genes as another yet returns half its participation ratio. Second, of the
perturbation environments audited, only two contain at least 8000 cells and
only 101 contain at least 500, against detection floors of 125 and 8000 cells
measured here for hard and soft mechanism shifts. Third, the two modalities
fail in opposite directions, one supplying breadth without depth and the other
depth without breadth. No model is fitted at any step, so these conclusions
cannot be attributed to estimator failure.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\IEEEPARstart{C}{ausal} representation learning (CRL) seeks to recover a small
set of latent causal variables, together with the causal graph relating them,
from high-dimensional observations gathered under several experimental
conditions \cite{scholkopf2021}. The appeal for biomedical data is immediate.
A single-cell CRISPR screen measures tens of thousands of genes per cell
across thousands of genetic perturbations; a neuroimaging study measures
hundreds of brain regions across sites, subjects, and tasks. In both cases the
measured variables are widely believed to be surface manifestations of a much
smaller set of underlying mechanisms, which is precisely the structure CRL
assumes.

The theoretical foundations of the field are now well developed.
Identifiability results exist for linear mixing with hard interventions
\cite{squires2023}, for nonparametric mixing with unknown interventions
\cite{kugelgen2023}, for general nonlinear mixing \cite{buchholz2023}, and for
soft interventions \cite{zhang2023}. Each of these results is conditional.
Identifiability is guaranteed only when the data satisfy stated preconditions,
and the most consequential of these are not assumptions about the world but
requirements on the experimental design: how many distinct environments were
collected, and how much data each contains. Rajendran \emph{et al.}
\cite{rajendran2024} state the requirement in its bluntest form, showing that
recovering $n$ concepts requires at least $n+1$ environments in the favourable
case, and roughly $2n+1$ for variational formulations.

Against this theoretical progress, the empirical record is discouraging.
Gamella \emph{et al.} \cite{gamella2025} constructed a physical optical system
that satisfies the core CRL assumptions by design, with the underlying causal
factors known and controlled, and found that every representative method they
evaluated failed to recover them. In the transcriptomic setting,
Ahlmann-Eltze \emph{et al.} \cite{ahlmann2025} found that five foundation
models and two other deep learning models failed to outperform deliberately
simple linear baselines at predicting perturbation effects. Similar
conclusions have been reached by several independent evaluation frameworks
\cite{vollenweider2026,vinas2026}.

The response of the field to this record has been to build better methods and
better metrics. That response presupposes that the datasets used for
evaluation are capable of supporting the task. We ask whether they are.
Specifically, we treat the preconditions of the identifiability theorems as
measurable quantities and evaluate them directly on the datasets that CRL
papers use for empirical validation.

This is not a new kind of question, although it appears to be new in this
literature. Econometrics has for three decades distinguished between a model
that is wrong and data that cannot identify a model that is right, and has
developed pre-tests for the second case. Staiger and Stock \cite{staiger1997}
characterised the weak-instrument regime, Stock and Yogo \cite{stock2005} gave
usable diagnostics, and the associated rank-testing literature
\cite{kleibergen2006,robin2000,chen2019} provides formal machinery for asking
whether a matrix that must have a given rank for identification actually does.
What we present here is a pre-test of the same kind, adapted to
multi-environment CRL and applied retrospectively to a benchmark corpus.

Our contributions are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
\item We define a precondition audit: a set of measurements, computed from
data alone before any model is fitted, that reports whether a
multi-environment dataset can in principle support latent causal
identification. The audit requires no ground-truth latents, no trained model,
and no assumption about the mixing function. It is set out in full as
Procedure~\ref{proc:audit}.

\item We report the first estimates, to our knowledge, of the observed
dimension of the control-cell covariance for the Replogle K562 and RPE1,
Norman, and Frangieh perturbation screens. Values recurring in the literature,
such as 50 principal components, 100 principal components, or a latent
dimension set equal to the number of targets, are user-chosen pipeline
hyperparameters rather than estimator outputs.

\item We tabulate per-environment sample size against measured detection
floors across eight datasets and two modalities, and show that transcriptomic
and neuroimaging benchmarks violate the preconditions in complementary and
opposite ways.

\item We show that the participation ratio, a dimension estimator in common
use, is not robust to rank-based transformation of zero-inflated count data,
and characterise the artefact quantitatively. This is a methodological caution
of independent interest to anyone estimating dimensionality from single-cell
data.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Preconditions for Multi-Environment Causal Representation Learning}
\label{sec:precond}

\subsection{Setting}
We consider the standard multi-environment CRL setting. A latent vector
$\mathbf{z}$ of dimension $d$ is generated by a structural causal model. An
observation $\mathbf{x}$ of dimension $p$ is produced by a mixing map $g$, so
that $\mathbf{x} = g(\mathbf{z})$. Data are collected in $K$ environments
indexed by $e$, where each environment corresponds to an intervention on one
or more latent coordinates, or in the observational case to no intervention.
The goal is to recover $g$ up to a permissible ambiguity class, and to recover
the causal graph over $\mathbf{z}$.

Identifiability results in this setting differ in the mixing class they permit
and in the intervention type they assume, but they share a common structure: a
set of conditions under which the map from latents to observations can be
inverted uniquely. Three of these conditions are properties of the dataset
rather than of the estimator, and can therefore be checked before any method
is run.

\subsection{Precondition 1: environment count}
Identification requires that the environments jointly supply enough
independent constraints to pin down the latent coordinates. In the cleanest
statement of this requirement, recovering $n$ latent concepts requires at
least $n+1$ environments, and identification via variational objectives
requires roughly $2n+1$ \cite{rajendran2024}. This precondition is pure
counting. It does not depend on the mixing function, the intervention type,
the noise model, or the sample size, and it can therefore be checked with
complete confidence for any dataset. If the number of environments is $K$, no
more than approximately $K-1$ latent directions can be resolved, whatever
method is applied.

\subsection{Precondition 2: per-environment sample size}
An environment contributes information only if the mechanism change it induces
is detectable above sampling noise. This is a power question, and unlike the
environment count it depends on the effect size and on the statistic used to
detect the change. Prospective power tooling for perturbation screens exists
and is well developed. PerturbPlan \cite{niu2026} derives an analytical power
formula for detecting perturbation-expression associations and provides an
interactive design application. That machinery targets per-gene differential
expression for experiments not yet run. The quantity relevant here is
different: the number of samples required to detect a change in the
second-order structure of the data, which is the object that CRL
identifiability arguments manipulate. We measure this floor directly
(Section~\ref{sec:floors}) and apply it retrospectively to datasets already
collected.

\subsection{Precondition 3: latent dimension within the identifiable regime}
The published theorems do not impose an upper bound on $d$. In practice,
however, every estimator and every test has a regime in which it behaves as
its theory predicts, and that regime is finite. We therefore measure two
things and keep them carefully distinct. The first is the observed dimension
of the data, defined as the effective dimension of the control-condition
covariance, which is a lower bound on the number of latent directions that
could be present. The second is the dimension range in which our detection
instrument is exact, established by simulation. We do not claim that CRL is
unidentifiable above some dimension; we claim that the observed dimension of
these datasets lies an order of magnitude outside the range in which our
instrument, and by extension the finite-sample behaviour of comparable
second-order procedures, has been verified.

\subsection{Relation to weak-identification pre-testing}
The audit is conceptually a pre-test in the sense of the weak-identification
literature. In instrumental-variable regression, the parameter of interest is
identified only if the first-stage coefficient matrix has full rank; when it
is close to rank-deficient, estimators remain computable and report finite
standard errors while being severely biased and having badly distorted
coverage \cite{staiger1997}. The recommended practice is to test the
identification condition before interpreting the estimate \cite{stock2005},
with formal rank tests supplying the machinery
\cite{kleibergen2006,robin2000,chen2019}. The situation in CRL is analogous.
Nothing prevents a CRL method from being fitted to a dataset whose
environments cannot identify the latent model; the method will converge,
report a representation, and score on a benchmark. The purpose of the audit is
to determine whether that score carries information about the method.

\section{Materials and Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Datasets}
We audit eight datasets spanning two modalities. Replogle K562 and RPE1
\cite{replogle2022} are genome-scale Perturb-seq screens using CRISPR
interference, comprising 1159 and 652 perturbation targets respectively.
Norman \cite{norman2019} is a CRISPR activation screen with 105 single-gene
targets measured over 5000 highly variable genes. Frangieh
\cite{frangieh2021} is a Perturb-CITE-seq screen in patient-derived melanoma,
assayed under three experimental arms (co-culture, control, and
interferon-$\gamma$), each containing 248 targets; the arms are audited
separately because the arm effect would otherwise dominate any pooled
statistic. The Human Connectome Project \cite{vanessen2013} contributes seven
task conditions, and the Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange
\cite{dimartino2014} contributes fourteen acquisition sites.

The two neuroimaging corpora are included for the environment-count and
per-environment-sample-size analyses, where the comparison is informative, and
are excluded from the covariance-dimension comparison, where it is not.
Neither has a control condition analogous to unperturbed cells, so their
spectra are computed over pooled frames and are not commensurable with the
Perturb-seq control spectra. We further note explicitly that ABIDE sites are
measurement shifts rather than interventions, so the attrition curve for ABIDE
is a descriptive analogue and not an interventional one.

\subsection{Environment attrition}
For each dataset we count the number of environments, and the number surviving
thresholds of 125, 500, and 8000 samples per environment. For transcriptomic
screens a sample is a cell assigned to that perturbation; for neuroimaging a
sample is a timepoint. We report the median environment size and the full
quartile range. No filtering, pooling, or reweighting is applied.

\subsection{Observed dimension of the control covariance}
Dimension is estimated from control cells only, so that no perturbation effect
can contribute. We compute three estimators from the eigenvalue spectrum of
the control covariance: the participation ratio, the effective rank defined as
the exponential of the spectral entropy, and the number of components required
to reach 95 per cent of the variance. The three disagree systematically, by a
factor of roughly 1.8 on K562, and we report all three throughout and treat
their spread as the honest uncertainty rather than selecting the most
favourable.

Two properties of these estimates require emphasis. They are sample-size
dependent, and they are lower bounds. Raising the control sample from 2000 to
8000 cells raises the K562 participation ratio from 455.5 to 552.5 and the
effective rank from 722.8 to 885.8. All cross-dataset comparisons are
therefore made at a matched control sample size, which is stated in every
table. Where the number of control cells is smaller than the number of genes,
the effective rank and the 95 per cent variance count are bounded by the
sample size and become noise-contaminated; in that regime, which applies to
all Frangieh arms, only the participation ratio remains comparable across
datasets, and we flag those rows explicitly.

As an independent check on whether the observed dimension is inflated by
measurement noise, we compare the participation ratio against the point at
which the empirical spectrum crosses the Marchenko--Pastur edge for a
pure-noise matrix of the same shape \cite{marchenko1967}. This comparison is
valid only where the covariance is of full rank, which holds for K562 and RPE1
and fails for the remaining datasets, where the aspect ratio pushes the noise
edge far above the bulk. We therefore use it as corroboration where it is
available and state plainly where it is not.

\subsection{Detection floors}
\label{sec:floors}
The per-environment sample-size floor is measured rather than assumed. We
simulate a multi-environment linear structural model with known latent
dimension and a known intervention, and test the null hypothesis that the rank
of the difference between two environment covariances does not exceed a fixed
value, using the composite-null bootstrap rank test of Chen and Fang
\cite{chen2019}. The test statistic is the sum of trailing squared singular
values with the rank held fixed at the null value, which removes all tuning
constants from the procedure. We use a significance level of 0.05 and 500
bootstrap replicates throughout.

Calibration was verified before the floors were read off. Under a true rank of
zero the empirical rejection rate stayed below the upper confidence band in
every configuration and every seed. Under interventions on source nodes, which
place the truth in the interior of the null, the rejection rate was exactly
zero in all six configurations tested. Under interventions on non-source
nodes, which place the truth on the boundary of the null, the rejection rate
was 0.097 unstandardised and 0.083 standardised, roughly 1.9 and 1.7 times
nominal. This excess did not shrink across a fortyfold range of sample sizes
and is therefore structural, reflecting the known degradation of regularity
conditions at the rank boundary, rather than a finite-sample effect. We report
it rather than gate on it.

Reading power off these curves gives a floor of 125 samples per environment
for hard interventions and 8000 for soft interventions, a ratio of roughly
sixty-four. The distinction matters because CRISPR interference produces
knockdown rather than deletion, and knockdown is a soft intervention by
construction. We report both floors against every dataset rather than
committing to one.

We additionally measured the range of mixing nonlinearity within which the
test remains exact, parameterised by a curvature scale. That range is not a
single number: it shrinks with latent dimension, from a curvature scale above
0.1 at latent dimension 10 to 0.02 at latent dimension 20. Combined with the
dimension results below, this is the reason we did not apply the rank test to
real data. Curvature can only push the test toward more rejection, so a
non-rejection would remain a strong claim, but a rejection would be confounded
with mixing curvature and could not be interpreted. The test is used here
solely to establish the envelope; every number reported on real data is a
count or an eigenvalue.

\subsection{Preprocessing sensitivity}
To determine whether the dimension estimates are properties of the data or of
the normalisation pipeline, we recomputed all estimators under three
transformations: the matrices as distributed, per-gene standardisation, and
per-gene rank-inverse-normal transformation. A fourth arm applying a logarithm
followed by standardisation was specified but refused at run time. A loader
probe found that none of the non-zero entries in any of the six matrices was
integral, with maxima near 5 and per-entry means below 1, indicating that all
six matrices had already been log-transformed upstream. Applying a further
logarithm would have constituted a double transformation, and the arm was
emitted as null with the reason recorded rather than reported as a legitimate
preprocessing choice.

This is itself a finding worth stating. None of the loaders used by the
benchmark pipelines we examined records what normalisation was applied, and
the transformation history of these widely used matrices must be inferred from
their numerical fingerprint. We treat this as a limitation of the benchmark
ecosystem rather than of our audit, but it constrains what any dimension
estimate on these data can claim.

\subsection{Reproducibility}
All estimators were validated against a fixed-seed synthetic case with pinned
expected values before being applied to real data, and the gate cannot report
success while those values are unpinned. The complete pipeline was executed
independently on two machines with different operating systems, BLAS backends,
and library versions; the fixed-seed synthetic outputs agreed to fifteen
decimal places. Every result artefact records library versions, platform, BLAS
backend, and a full-precision fingerprint of the input matrix, so that a
difference in data can never be mistaken for version drift.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Environment attrition}
Table~\ref{tab:attrition} reports the number of environments surviving each
sample-size threshold. Two features stand out. First, only two perturbation
environments in the entire corpus, one in K562 and one in RPE1, contain at
least 8000 cells, the floor for detecting a soft mechanism shift. Second, the
attrition between 125 and 500 cells is severe: 899 K562 environments clear 125
cells but only 27 clear 500, and no Frangieh environment in any of the three
arms reaches 500. Across the six perturbation datasets, 101 environments of
2164 distinct targets contain at least 500 cells.

Fig.~\ref{fig:breadth}(b) shows the same attrition as survival curves. The six
perturbation datasets fall as a tight bundle and reach the floor together; the
two neuroimaging corpora are flat across the same range. The separation is not
gradual but categorical.

This is not an accident of the data but a visible design decision.
Genome-scale screens deliberately distribute a fixed cell budget across as
many targets as possible, buying breadth at the cost of depth. That decision
is entirely appropriate for the purpose those screens were designed to serve.
It is adverse for latent causal identification, which requires each
environment to individually resolve a mechanism change.

\subsection{Observed dimension of the control covariance}
Table~\ref{tab:dimension} reports the three dimension estimators at a matched
control sample of 2000 cells. The participation ratio ranges from 224 to 456
and the 95 per cent variance count from 496 to 1725. The largest single
eigenvalue accounts for between 2.1 and 4.1 per cent of the total variance,
which is the most informative number in the table: there is no dominant
direction. Variance is distributed across hundreds of components rather than
concentrated in a handful with noise around them. These data are
high-dimensional rather than low-dimensional and noisy.

Fig.~\ref{fig:dimension}(a) plots the three estimators against the envelope in
which our detection instrument is verified exact, shaded near the floor of the
axis. Every bar for every dataset sits one to two orders of magnitude above
it. Bars marked with a dagger are those for which the number of control cells
is below the number of genes, so effective rank and 95 per cent variance are
bounded by $n$ rather than by the data.

Raising the control sample to 8000 cells raises every estimate, K562 to 552.5,
RPE1 to 261.6 and Norman to 248.1, confirming that the values in
Table~\ref{tab:dimension} are lower bounds.

\subsection{Dimension is not an artefact of feature count}
The immediate objection to Table~\ref{tab:dimension} is that the estimates
simply track the number of measured genes. They do not. Norman is measured
over 5000 genes and K562 over 1158, a factor of more than four, yet at an
identical control sample of 8000 cells Norman returns a participation ratio of
248.1 against K562's 552.5, less than half, and in the opposite direction to
the objection. Across a thirty-sixfold range of feature counts the
participation ratio spans only about a factor of two, and does so
non-monotonically. Whatever the observed dimension is measuring, it is not the
width of the matrix.

Fig.~\ref{fig:dimension}(b) makes the comparison directly: the arrow runs from
K562 to Norman, from fewer genes to more and from higher dimension to lower.

\subsection{Spectrum shape and Marchenko--Pastur corroboration}
The K562 and RPE1 control spectra decay smoothly over three to four orders of
magnitude with no knee, which is inconsistent with a low-dimensional signal
embedded in isotropic noise and consistent with the broad eigenvalue
distributions reported for other high-dimensional biological systems
\cite{stringer2019}. For these two datasets the covariance is of full rank and
the Marchenko--Pastur crossing can be computed; it agrees with the
participation ratio to within 7 to 28 per cent (K562: 522 against 456 at
$n = 2000$, and 517 against 553 at $n = 8000$; RPE1: 284 against 234, and 336
against 262). Two estimators resting on entirely different principles converge
on the same order of magnitude.

For the remaining datasets the number of genes far exceeds the number of
control cells, the noise edge is pushed roughly twenty times above the bulk,
and the crossing returns values between 2 and 3 against participation ratios
of 415 to 436. The comparison is uninformative there and we do not use it. The
datasets on which this corroboration is unavailable are those with the most
severe sample-size limitation, which is the second finding of this audit
reappearing in a different form.

Fig.~\ref{fig:dimension}(c) plots the two estimates against each other for the
four cases in which both are available. All four fall inside a band of plus or
minus twenty-five per cent around the identity line, and the residual scatter
has no systematic direction.

\subsection{Preprocessing sensitivity}
Table~\ref{tab:preproc} reports the dimension estimators under the three
usable preprocessing arms. The participation ratio is not invariant, and the
pattern is instructive. Under standardisation it rises, substantially so where
the number of genes exceeds the number of cells, because standardisation
upweights low-variance genes that carry mostly noise. Under
rank-inverse-normal transformation it collapses, to values between 1.4 and 2.2
on the four sparsest datasets.

That collapse is an artefact with an identifiable mechanism, not a competing
estimate. The rank-inverse-normal transform is applied per gene; where a large
fraction of entries are exactly zero, those entries tie and map to a single
value, and the transform manufactures one dominant direction reflecting the
detection pattern rather than expression covariance. The leading eigenvalue's
variance share under this arm increases monotonically with the zero fraction
across all six datasets without exception, from 0.118 at a zero fraction of
0.397 (K562) to 0.832 at 0.911 (Norman). Because the participation ratio is
quadratic in the eigenvalues, a single direction of that size pins it near its
reciprocal.

Critically, the 95 per cent variance count is essentially unchanged under the
same transform on the two least sparse datasets: 800 against 798 for K562, and
496 against 499 for RPE1. The same data, under a transform that destroys one
estimator, returns the other estimator to within a few components. The
conclusion of Section~\ref{sec:results} therefore holds under all three
preprocessing arms, and the appropriate methodological caution, that the
participation ratio should not be used alone on zero-inflated count data
subjected to rank-based transformation, is stated rather than concealed.

Fig.~\ref{fig:preproc}(a) shows the mechanism directly: the variance share of
the leading eigendirection under the rank-inverse-normal arm rises
monotonically with the fraction of zero entries, without exception across the
six datasets. Fig.~\ref{fig:preproc}(b) shows the consequence for the two
estimators side by side, with the 95 per cent variance count for K562 and RPE1
moving by two and three components respectively while the participation ratio
falls by an order of magnitude.

\subsection{Breadth and depth are dissociated across modalities}
Reading Tables~\ref{tab:attrition} and~\ref{tab:dimension} together produces
the structural result of this audit. The transcriptomic screens supply many
environments with very little data in each: K562 offers 1159 perturbations at
a median of 165 cells apiece. The neuroimaging corpora supply the opposite:
HCP offers seven task conditions at a median of 32384 timepoints, and ABIDE
fourteen sites at a median of 4930, with every environment clearing the
hard-intervention floor comfortably.

Fig.~\ref{fig:breadth}(a) places every dataset on a plane whose axes are the
two preconditions: number of environments on one axis, median samples per
environment on the other. The region satisfying both simultaneously is shaded.
It is empty. The perturbation screens occupy the lower right, the neuroimaging
corpora the upper left, and no dataset in the corpus lies in the quadrant that
identification requires. The vertical boundary is drawn at 225 environments,
the requirement implied by the smallest observed dimension in the corpus,
which is the most permissive placement the data allow.

Latent causal identification requires both. It requires at least $d+1$
environments to resolve $d$ latent directions \cite{rajendran2024}, and it
requires each environment to individually supply enough samples to detect the
mechanism change. Perturb-seq satisfies the first and fails the second; task
and site fMRI satisfy the second and fail the first by an enormous margin.
Fourteen ABIDE sites cap the resolvable latent dimension at thirteen, against
an observed dimension of several hundred in the comparable transcriptomic
case. Neither modality, as currently collected, supplies both preconditions
simultaneously.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the audit establishes}
The audit establishes that the datasets most commonly used to evaluate
multi-environment CRL fail the preconditions of the identifiability theorems
on two grounds that require no modelling assumptions. The observed dimension
of the control covariance is one to two orders of magnitude above the range in
which our detection instrument is verified exact, and per-environment sample
sizes are far below the floors at which a soft mechanism shift becomes
detectable. Because no model is fitted anywhere in the audit, these
conclusions cannot be attributed to a failure of estimation. They are counts
and eigenvalues computed on public data.

The immediate consequence is interpretive. When a CRL method scores poorly on
K562, the result is consistent with the method being inadequate and equally
consistent with the data being unable to support any method. The benchmark
does not distinguish these. That ambiguity is not resolved by improving the
metric, because the metric is not what is failing.

We suggest that this offers a parsimonious reading of the empirical record
described in Section~\ref{sec:intro}. The methods evaluated by Gamella
\emph{et al.} \cite{gamella2025} failed on a system built to satisfy the
assumptions, which is genuine evidence about methods; the failures reported on
transcriptomic benchmarks \cite{ahlmann2025,vollenweider2026,vinas2026} are at
least partly attributable to the data. We state this as a hypothesis the audit
makes available, not as a measured result, since no method is fitted here.

A competing explanation exists and should be stated. Unweighted error metrics
and control-referenced deltas can reward prediction of the perturbation mean
when biological signal is sparse, so reported failures may partly reflect
metric insensitivity rather than data insufficiency \cite{vollenweider2026}.
The two are not exclusive, and the audit does not adjudicate between them. We
note only that on K562 ridge regression reaches 0.226 against a ceiling of
0.528 under the same unweighted metric, which shows that the metric is not
blind to signal that is present.

Figs.~\ref{fig:breadth} to~\ref{fig:preproc} and
Tables~\ref{tab:attrition} to~\ref{tab:preproc} present the same three
measurements from complementary directions, and no reading of them requires a
fitted model.

\subsection{What the audit does not establish}
Three claims that the results might appear to license are not made, and we set
them out explicitly.

First, we measure the observed dimension of the control covariance, not the
latent dimension. The observed dimension bounds the latent dimension from
below under the assumption that the mixing map does not destroy dimension,
which is standard but is an assumption. We use the word ``observed''
throughout for this reason.

Second, we do not claim that these datasets lie outside the regime in which
CRL is identifiable. The published theorems state no upper bound on the latent
dimension. The range of 20 to 40 referenced here is the verified operating
envelope of our own instrument, established by simulation, and the defensible
statement is that the observed dimension exceeds the envelope in which that
instrument is exact.

Third, the floor of 8000 cells is derived from our simulator under a specific
statistic, and its transfer to real CRISPR interference experiments is an
extrapolation. The floor of 125 cells is far more conservative and is cleared
by 899 of 1159 K562 environments. We report both and note that the qualitative
conclusion for Frangieh, where no environment in any arm reaches 500 cells,
does not depend on which floor is adopted.

\subsection{Partial identifiability}
The strongest available counter-argument is that full identification is not
the only useful goal. Recent theory permits partial identification of a
limited subset of latent concepts from a sublinear or logarithmic number of
environments, which would substantially relax the environment-count
precondition. We regard this as the most promising direction for making CRL
work on data of this kind, and we do not compete with it.

It does not, however, rescue the datasets audited here. The per-environment
sample-size finding is untouched by it: two environments in the corpus contain
enough cells to detect a soft mechanism shift regardless of how many
environments the theory requires. The dimension finding continues to constrain
how finely any recovered subspace can be resolved. Partial identifiability
changes what one should aim for; it does not change what the data contain.

\subsection{Recommendation}
We suggest that evaluations of multi-environment causal methods report a short
precondition summary alongside their results: the number of environments, the
distribution of samples per environment against a stated detection floor, and
an estimate of observed dimension with the sample size at which it was
computed. This costs a table. It would allow a reader to distinguish a method
that failed from a dataset that could not have supported success, which at
present no published benchmark permits.

Procedure~\ref{proc:audit} sets that recommendation out in full. It is a
reporting protocol rather than an estimation method: every step is a count, an
eigenvalue, or a statement of provenance, and none of it requires a fitted
model or ground-truth latents. Applied to the eight datasets audited here it
produces Tables~\ref{tab:attrition} to~\ref{tab:preproc} and
Figs.~\ref{fig:breadth} to~\ref{fig:preproc} in their entirety.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{Precondition audit for a multi-environment dataset.}
\label{proc:audit}
\footnotesize
\textbf{Input:} feature matrix $\mathbf{X}$ ($n$ samples $\times$ $p$
features), environment labels, and a designated control or observational
environment where one exists.\\
\textbf{Output:} a precondition report giving the environment count, the
attrition curve, the observed dimension with its uncertainty, and a verdict
for each precondition.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Enumerate environments.} Record $K$ and the sample size $n_e$ of
each. Do not pool, filter, or reweight. Where environments are nested, audit
each level separately and state which level is treated as the environment.
\item \textbf{Classify the environments.} State for each whether it
corresponds to an intervention or to a measurement shift. Measurement shifts
supply no mechanism variation and must be reported as descriptive only.
\item \textbf{Fix the detection floors before inspecting the data.} Adopt
published power figures for the assay, or measure floors by simulation
calibrated to the statistic that would be used. Report floors for both hard
and soft interventions and state which applies.
\item \textbf{Compute the attrition curve.} For a set of thresholds, count the
environments at or above each. Report the median and quartiles of $n_e$, and
absolute counts as well as fractions.
\item \textbf{Isolate the control condition.} Estimate dimension on
unperturbed samples only. Where no control exists, say so; a spectrum over
pooled samples is not comparable to a control spectrum.
\item \textbf{Estimate observed dimension at two matched sample sizes.} At
each, report the participation ratio, the effective rank, the number of
components reaching 95 per cent of variance, and the leading eigenvalue's
share. Compare across datasets only at identical $n$, and report every
estimator.
\item \textbf{Flag rank deficiency.} Where $n < p$, mark the row. Effective
rank and the variance count are then bounded by $n$ rather than by the data;
only the participation ratio remains comparable.
\item \textbf{Corroborate against a noise null.} Where the covariance is of
full rank, report the Marchenko--Pastur edge crossing for a matched-shape
noise matrix. Where it is not, state that the comparison is unavailable rather
than reporting the value it returns.
\item \textbf{Test preprocessing sensitivity.} Probe the input first, since
the integrality of non-zero entries reveals whether an upstream transform has
been applied, and refuse any arm that would transform twice. Repeat step 6
under at least three admissible transformations and report the spread as
uncertainty.
\item \textbf{Report a verdict for each precondition.} State whether the bound
used is a published theorem, a measured floor, or the verified envelope of the
instrument employed. A measured envelope must never be described as a property
of the theory.
\end{enumerate}
\textit{No model is fitted at any step, and no ground-truth latent variables
are required.}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Limitations}
Our dimension estimators are linear. Nonlinear intrinsic-dimension estimators
such as TwoNN would provide useful corroboration and typically return
substantially lower values on single-cell data; we did not run them, and we
flag in advance that a disagreement should not be resolved by preferring the
more convenient answer.

We report uncorrected estimates at two matched sample sizes rather than
applying finite-sample bias corrections. Corrections of this kind require
assuming a noise model and act to reduce the estimates, so an uncorrected
lower bound with the sample size stated is the more conservative posture.

The preprocessing provenance of all six transcriptomic matrices is
undocumented in the loaders we examined and was inferred from numerical
fingerprints. We did not attempt to reconstruct the upstream pipelines.

The detection floors are simulator outputs under a linear structural model,
and their transfer to real CRISPR interference biology is the weakest link in
the sample-size argument.

Finally, we did not apply the rank test to real data, for the reason set out
in Section~\ref{sec:floors}. Under nonlinear mixing the test over-rejects, so
a rejection on real data would be confounded with mixing curvature. Every
real-data number reported here is a count or an eigenvalue, which is why the
conclusions of the audit survive that limitation.

%=============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
We have audited eight benchmark datasets used to evaluate multi-environment
causal representation learning against the preconditions their identifiability
theorems impose, using only measurements computed from the data before any
model is fitted. The observed dimension of the control covariance is several
hundred by every estimator we applied, and is not explained by feature count,
by noise, or by preprocessing choice. Per-environment sample sizes fall far
below measured detection floors, with only two environments in the corpus
clearing the soft-intervention floor and none of the 744 Frangieh environments
reaching even 500 cells. Transcriptomic and neuroimaging benchmarks fail these
preconditions in opposite and complementary directions, one supplying breadth
without depth and the other depth without breadth.

The practical implication is that current benchmark scores for CRL on these
datasets are not evidence about the methods being scored. Progress requires
either data collected with identification in mind, meaning deeper environments
or designs that trade target breadth for cells per target, or theory that
identifies less from what is available. Distinguishing those two paths
requires knowing which precondition binds, which is what an audit of this kind
supplies.
